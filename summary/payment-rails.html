Neobank stack · deep dive

Payment rails

Where a clean internal model meets other people's guarantees — UK and USA, from the settlement mechanics down to the return codes that decide whether you keep network access.

18 Aug 20262 markets7 rails4 archetypes19 sources

Almost every expensive mistake in this layer traces to a single conceptual error: treating authorisation, clearing and settlement as the same event. They are three different things, at three different times, with three different failure modes — and a ledger that models only one of them will be wrong in a way that surfaces months later as a break nobody can explain.

So start there.

01The tail is the design constraint

Every rail can take money back. What separates them is how long they have to do it.

Teams build the happy path and then discover the reversal branch in production. These windows should be sized before you write the state machine, because they decide how long you must retain the ability to reverse a posting cleanly — and how much exposure sits on your balance sheet at any moment.

02United Kingdom

Instant to the customer, netted between the banks, and prefunded at the central bank.

Faster Payments — the settlement lag nobody sees

The customer sees money instantly. The banks settle hours later, on a net basis. The bridge between those two facts is prefunding: every participating institution holds a cash deposit covering its net position in a segregated, interest-bearing account at the Bank of England. That eliminates settlement risk — a failing participant cannot take others down — at the cost of tying up liquidity.

“Direct” and “participant” are two different questions

The terminology trips almost everyone, because “direct” describes the connection, while settlement is a separate axis entirely. You can be directly connected to the scheme without settling in your own name — and that middle model is the one most people actually want.

The 2026 change worth re-running your numbers for

In July 2026 Pay.UK went live with a flexible liquidity model for Faster Payments Net Sender Caps, explicitly to reduce prefunding requirements and lower the cost of direct access for non-bank payment providers.

Prefunding has been the single biggest economic barrier to direct participation. If you evaluated direct access before mid-2026 and rejected it on liquidity cost, that calculation has changed.

DCNSP is the model most people mean when they say “direct access” without a banking licence. It was introduced specifically to widen participation: you get your own real-time connection and set your own limits, but you need neither a full banking licence nor a Bank of England settlement account, and the liquidity and risk-management burden stays with the sponsor. The gain over indirect access is that you are no longer behind an aggregator's API — which removes them as a single point of failure in the payment path.

Bacs — a three-day cycle and a message taxonomy

Bacs is not an integration; it is a product. Three days (input, processing, entry) and four return services you must handle:

ServiceWhat it tells you
AUDDISProblems setting up a mandate, or the payer cancelling the setup. Responses arrive on Day 2
ADDACSAmendments and cancellations of mandates initiated by the customer
ARUDDReturned and unpaid Direct Debits
DDICAIndemnity claims — the payer's bank clawing money back under the Guarantee

Two traps that catch people. Reason code 1 means the payer explicitly cancelled — re-presenting after that produces an indemnity claim, so retry logic must branch on reason code, never on "it failed". And the eight DDICA reason codes cover everything from the amount differing from the advance notice to no instruction held to a fraudulent signature — each one a reversal you must accept, possibly months later.

CHAPS — already migrated, and it's the template

CHAPS matters beyond high-value payments because it shows where all UK payments are heading:

  • 19 June 2023 — CHAPS migrated to ISO 20022 messaging.
  • 28 April 2025 — the Bank of England's new RTGS core ledger and settlement engine went live.
  • 1 May 2025 — enhanced data became mandatory: Purpose Codes and LEIs on domestic interbank payments (pacs.009 CORE and certain pacs.008), and Purpose Codes on property transactions.
  • 2026 — the Bank is consulting on extending settlement hours toward near-24x7, which would allow more frequent net settlement and lower prefunding costs across every rail.

The direction is unambiguous: structured, mandatory, machine-readable data attached to payments. Model your internal messages on ISO 20022 semantics now, even where today's rail doesn't require it.

03United States

Cheap and slow, or instant and irreversible — and neither of the instant rails can pull.

The number that can end your ACH access

Nacha's Unauthorized Return Rate Threshold is 0.5%, measured across R05, R07, R10, R29 and R51. That is not a target — it is a threshold with enforcement behind it.

Which makes unauthorised-return rate a metric your risk team must watch continuously, and makes clear consent capture and unambiguous billing descriptors an engineering concern rather than a marketing one. Same Day ACH's per-entry limit also rises from $1m to $10m on 17 September 2027.

Credit-push only is the design fact that matters. Neither instant rail can pull funds. Any product that needs to debit a customer — subscriptions, loan repayments, top-ups — still needs ACH or cards underneath, with request-for-payment as at best a nudge. That is the exact inverse of the UK, where Direct Debit makes pull the default.

And because the two networks differ in reach, limits and price, a US neobank needs a routing layer that chooses per transaction on cost, speed and reachability — not a hardcoded rail.

04What this forces you to build

Five subsystems that are not optional, in the order they tend to bite.

1 · A state machine with a long tail

initiated → risk-checked → authorised → submitted → accepted → settled
     │            │             │            │           │
     └─ blocked   └─ rejected   └─ failed    └─ returned   └─ reversed
                                              days later   months later

The happy path is the easy half. The reversal branch is where the money is.

2 · A liquidity management system, if you go direct

Prefunding is not a one-off deposit; it is a continuously managed position with caps, monitoring and top-up automation. This is the part teams underestimate when they model direct participation as a purely technical project — and it is what the July 2026 Net Sender Cap change made cheaper.

3 · Rail-aware retry

Branch on reason code, never on "it failed". Retrying an R10, or a Bacs reason code 1, is not a bug that costs you one payment — it is a compliance event that counts against a threshold.

4 · Cut-offs and value dating as first-class concepts

Every rail has a cut-off, and a payment submitted after it takes a different value date. Customers experience that as "the money vanished for a day", and support experiences it as a ticket you cannot answer without modelling value date explicitly.

5 · Reconciliation inputs, per rail

Every adapter must emit the statement side of the reconciliation as well as the payment side. Building both together is far cheaper than retrofitting — and reconciliation is the one subsystem both the FCA and the FDIC independently decided you must be able to prove.

05Four payment archetypes, with the vendors

The same rails, reached four different ways. What changes is where settlement happens — and therefore what you own.

Follow the double-bordered box down each column. That is the account the money actually settles into, and it decides everything above it: who you contract with, what you must build, and whose incident becomes your outage.

These are not exclusive. Most real neobanks run two at once — a UK bank with a US programme sits in columns one and three simultaneously, with entirely separate reconciliation obligations on each. Monzo is exactly that: a direct participant at home, with Galileo behind its US business.

The questions to ask

  1. For each rail: are we direct, sponsored or indirect — and what breaks if the sponsor exits?
  2. Does our ledger model authorisation, clearing and settlement as three events?
  3. What is our unauthorised ACH return rate, and who watches it against the 0.5% threshold?
  4. Does retry logic branch on reason code, or on failure?
  5. Can we accept a Bacs indemnity claim arriving a year later and reverse it cleanly?
  6. Who manages the prefunded position, and what happens when it is exhausted mid-day?
  7. Is our internal message model ISO 20022-shaped?
  8. What routes a US payment between ACH, RTP and FedNow — a decision, or a hardcode?
