How a neobank is actually built

Ten layers, four decisions, three archetypes — condensed from a 25-page research wiki covering Monzo, Chime, Revolut, SoFi, Starling, Zopa, Varo, Remitly and others.

18 Aug 202610 banks2 markets103 sources~9 min read

The short version

A neobank is a thin, fast product layer over a slow, regulated money layer. Nearly every interesting engineering decision is about where you draw the line between them.

A founding team makes four choices, and everything else follows: what are you licensed as, do you own the ledger, who authorises your card transactions, and is financial crime a vendor or a platform. Each is usually treated as procurement. Each is really architecture.

The licence decision is load-bearing because it decides what a balance means. A deposit at a licensed bank is a liability on your own balance sheet. An e-money balance is a claim against safeguarded funds held elsewhere. A sponsor-bank fintech balance is a position inside somebody else's omnibus account. Identical UI; three different correctness requirements.

Six findings

  1. Two regulators converged on the same requirement, independently.Britain arrived via safeguarding rules (FCA PS25/12: daily reconciliation, monthly returns, 48-hour resolution packs, from 7 May 2026). America arrived via a bankruptcy that left roughly $60–90m of customer money unaccounted for (the FDIC's proposed post-Synapse rule: near real-time reconciliation of every fintech partner account). Both now demand that reconciliation be continuous and provable. It is the one subsystem to build first in either market — and the one no vendor can build for you, because it encodes your specific relationships.
  2. One bank answered the cloud-concentration question by building a second bank.Monzo Stand-in is a complete backup banking platform on a different cloud — 18 services on GCP against ~3,000 on AWS, at roughly 1% of the primary platform's cost, covering card spend, ATM, transfers, balance and card freeze. Logic is reimplemented rather than shared, so one bug cannot take both down, and real customers run on it continuously in testing mode. The hard part is not the failover but the return path: "advices" applied verbatim, deduplicated, tolerant of arriving in any order. Every regulator asks this question; this is the only public answer that is testable rather than aspirational.
  3. Vertical integration came back.Chime built its own payments processor, ChimeCore, and has run 100% of its users' credit-card transactions on it since late 2024, citing lower maintenance cost and less third-party dependence in its S-1. Its core financial datastore, CoreDB, is a MySQL cluster on AWS RDS holding 40TB and serving 30,000 connections — conventional technology, run extremely well: they moved it from MySQL 5 to 8 with a three-cluster blue/green and five minutes of downtime. SoFi reached the same place by acquisition, owning both Galileo and the Cyberbank core and selling them to competitors.
  4. Renting your core is now respectable at tier one.JPMorgan launched Chase UK on 10x SuperCore; Zopa launched a current-account beta on Thought Machine Vault in about ten months. That removes "regulators won't allow a cloud-native core" from the argument entirely. The lock-in simply moves — to the vendor's product-definition language.
  5. UK and USA fraud architecture diverge more than anything else.Since October 2024 UK firms must reimburse most authorised push payment fraud up to £85,000, split 50/50 between sending and receiving institution, with the burden of proving gross negligence on the firm. There is no US equivalent. A British neobank is therefore liable for money its own customer chose to send a fraudster — which makes outbound scam detection a P&L line and inbound mule detection directly monetised.
  6. Monzo's fraud platform is the most copyable public design in the sector.Controls run as pure functions in Starlark, so they are safely back-testable against history without touching live traffic, and every execution logs its input features, decision and version to BigQuery. The models behind them are now public too: a multi-task network with three shared layers feeding three task heads, whose output joins the original features in a LightGBM model — about 30% better than the LightGBM baseline, concentrated in rare fraud subtypes. And where an LLM appears, it is deliberately a feature extractor, never the decider: it turns customer questionnaire responses into structured signals that a calibrated supervised model scores. Live result: +20% fraud caught, with fewer legitimate payments sent to human review.

Three archetypes

Every neobank in the study is one of these. Which one you are is decided by the licence and the ledger.

ArchetypeOwnsRentsWhoThe cost
A · Full-stack bankLicence, ledger, fincrime platformCommodity edges only — documents, sanctions lists, bureau dataMonzo, Starling, RevolutEnormous. Monzo runs on the order of 1,600–2,800 microservices and had to build its own RPC framework to make that tractable
B · Platform tenantLicence, product, risk modelsThe core ledger — Vault, SuperCore, TransactZopa, Chase UK, Varo, AtomThe vendor's release train becomes your roadmap; their DSL becomes your migration cost
C · On a sponsor bankProduct, risk, sometimes a sub-ledgerThe charter, the deposits, the railsChime, Current, DaveThinnest moat, and since Synapse the most exposed posture with regulators

Chime is the interesting case because it fits none of them cleanly. It has no charter and never sought one, yet it insourced the fee-bearing technology directly above the regulated boundary. That is a genuinely distinct third path — own the technology without owning the licence — and the one most likely to be copied.

Where the two markets stop being the same problem

The application layer ports. Nothing underneath does.

DimensionUnited KingdomUnited States

Two consequences worth internalising. Because instant transfer is free and universal in Britain, "instant transfer for a fee" — a real US neobank revenue line — is unsellable there. And because interchange is capped, UK challengers monetise through lending and subscriptions instead, which is why Monzo's and Revolut's machine-learning priorities lead with credit and financial crime while US neobanks optimise card approval rates.

If you port a US stack to the UK

The gap you will find late is outbound scam detection. American stacks are built to stop fraud against the customer; British liability rules also make you responsible for fraud the customer was talked into committing on their own account. That requires in-flight intervention on the payment path, not post-hoc alerting.

Build or buy

Buy anything whose correctness is defined by somebody else. Build anything whose iteration speed is your loss rate or your margin.

LayerDefaultReopen the decision when
ReconciliationbuildNever. No vendor knows your break taxonomy

Fraud & AML detectionbuildBuy to launch. In the UK, APP liability makes it material immediately

What sits behind this

A wiki that maintains itself, and a backlog of the questions nobody has answered.

The full research is 25 interlinked pages — ten layer analyses, ten bank profiles, a vendor landscape, and cross-market comparisons — with every non-obvious claim carrying a confidence tag: confirmed when a company or regulator said it, reported for credible third parties, inferred when deduced from job ads or customer lists. Anything older than about eighteen months is dated, because a 2020 fact driving a 2026 recommendation is the most dangerous thing in a document like this.

Four skills and two scheduled jobs keep it current: a daily scout that sweeps banks, vendors and regulators and adds at least three new questions per pass, and a weekly lint that hunts contradictions, stale claims and gaps.

Two caveats on the evidence

Most published vendor-comparison material in this sector is search-optimised content marketing. It is usable for building a shortlist and close to worthless as evidence; everything sourced that way is tagged accordingly and should be settled with a bake-off.

And the numbers that would actually settle the build-versus-buy arguments are not public: cost per card authorisation at scale, false-positive rates for a mature monitoring stack, all-in cost per verified account. The research backlog is pointed at exactly those gaps, alongside the single highest-value unknown — whether Chime's proprietary core holds the deposit sub-ledger or only card authorisation, which decides whether its model is replicable at all.
