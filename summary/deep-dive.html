The four layers you build

Reconciliation, rail adapters, KYC orchestration and fraud detection — with the real architectures behind them, the open-source packages that already exist, and the vendors worth paying.

18 Aug 20264 layers30+ packages139 sources

The build-vs-buy table in the main research marks four layers build. This is the argument for each, checked against what companies actually run and what software already exists. The general rule underneath: buy anything whose correctness is defined by somebody else — list data, document forensics, scheme connectivity — and build anything whose iteration speed is your loss rate or your margin.

One thing worth saying up front, because it changed while this research was being done: several of these layers now have credible open-source foundations that did not exist a few years ago. You are not choosing between a blank editor and a vendor contract.

01Reconciliation

The only layer with no trigger to reconsider. You can buy a matching engine; you cannot buy a break taxonomy.

Vendors in this space are real and mature — Duco, AutoRek, SmartStream TLM, Gresham Clareti. Read what they are built for and the mismatch is obvious: capital markets, prime brokerage, asset management, securities and cash. None was designed for a neobank reconciling a card processor, three rails, a sponsor bank and a safeguarding account against one product ledger, daily, with regulator-facing output.

The best-documented real system is Uber's.

The seven matching patterns

Run them as a rule chain, cheapest first. Pattern 6 is what UK e-money firms are now legally required to run daily; pattern 4 is what will eat your compute budget if you leave it unbounded.

#PatternWhere it applies
11:1 — exact on ID, amount, dateWires, single invoice payments with stable references
2N:1 — group internal, sum to one external creditCard network net settlement
31:N — sum of external equals one internal, within tolerancePartial payments, instalments
4N:M — bounded subset-sum searchB2B netting, cross-currency. Expensive — last resort only
5Time-window — tolerance defined per railAuth/capture/settle, timezone cut-offs
6Balance-level — internal sum equals external balance at a point in timeOmnibus accounts, safeguarding
7Exception — residual after the chainEverything left

The break taxonomy — the part no vendor can sell you

Three top-level classes (data quality, identifier, timing) are the right skeleton. The granularity that actually helps an operations team looks more like this, and it is specific to your rails and partners:

timing/
  timing.settlement-lag          expected, auto-close after N days
  timing.cutoff-crossed          posted after rail cut-off
  timing.auth-capture-gap        card lifecycle, not a break at all
identifier/
  identifier.reference-rewritten counterparty replaced our reference
  identifier.duplicate-key       same idempotency key, two postings
amount/
  amount.fx-rounding             within tolerance, auto-close
  amount.fee-undisclosed         PSP deducted a fee we did not model — revenue leak
  amount.partial-settlement      expect a sibling record
missing/
  missing.internal               external has it, we do not — money we cannot explain
  missing.external               we have it, the rail does not — in flight, or lost

The two marked in red are the ones that end companies. Everything else is operations. A break register without owner, age and amount is not a control — unaged breaks are exactly how a shortfall hides until it is too large to absorb.

The idempotency trap

The most common way a correct-looking system produces a phantom: a retried payout that reuses the same idempotency key books the same transfer twice. The defence is atomic multi-party posting — the whole transaction lands or none of it does — not an application-level duplicate check, which is itself subject to races.

02Rail adapters

Buy the access. Build the normalisation layer. And stop writing bitmap parsers — that work is solved.

The layer between scheme access and your ledger is always yours, because it encodes what a payment means in your product. Uber's version normalises 50+ PSP formats into one internal type. But the wire-format work underneath — ISO 8583, ISO 20022, Nacha ACH, Fedwire — has mature open-source implementations that most teams rediscover only after building worse ones.

For a US neobank the moov-io family alone covers most of the file-format surface. Given the UK's ISO 20022 direction — the NPA work narrowed to replacing Faster Payments, with the RPIB consultation opened in June 2026 — modelling your internal messages on ISO 20022 now is the cheap option.

What you build on top

Five parts, of which the state machine is the one teams under-build:

initiated → risk-checked → authorised → submitted → accepted → settled
     │            │             │            │           │
     └─ blocked   └─ rejected   └─ failed    └─ returned   └─ reversed
                                                days later   months later

ACH returns arrive with R-codes days after the fact. Direct Debit indemnity claims can arrive months later and are effectively unbounded. Both must reverse cleanly against the ledger, and both are the branch that gets written last and tested least.

The other four: one canonical money-movement event with rail specifics pushed into adapters; a corridor as configuration rather than a code branch; idempotency keys you choose, because the rail's identifier is exactly what gets rewritten; and every adapter also emitting the statement side of the reconciliation input — far cheaper built together than retrofitted.

For the long-running orchestration, Temporal is the current default. The instructive datapoint is not that Stripe uses it but that Coinbase migrated onto it specifically to delete a homegrown SAGA implementation — homegrown saga orchestration is the thing teams build, regret, and replace.

The question to ask any of them is not "do you support FPS" but "what happens when you have an incident, and what is my failover?" A single access provider is a single point of failure for all outbound money — and it is precisely the concentration risk DORA Article 29 asks about.

03KYC orchestration

You buy the signals. You build the router that decides which to gather, in what order, and what to do when one is down.

An onboarding funnel is a decision tree over unreliable third parties. Vendors have different coverage by geography and document type, so a multi-market neobank routes. Vendors fail — not rarely — and without a fallback chain their availability is your conversion rate. Alloy exists as a company because of exactly this, marketing 270+ data sources behind one vendor-neutral layer. The question is whether you rent that or own it.

OPA is the underrated choice here. "Policy separated from application code, returning structured decisions including risk scores, embeddable as a library" is precisely the model-versus-policy separation the architecture needs — and it is the closest widely-adopted analogue to what Monzo built for fraud, which is the next section.

yente matters for a different reason: it removes the assumption that screening must be SaaS. Open data, open matching engine, fully self-hosted, no customer data leaving your infrastructure. It does not remove the need for curated adverse-media and enhanced-diligence content — that is where ComplyAdvantage, Dow Jones and LexisNexis still earn their fee.

04Fraud & AML detection

Two public architectures define the state of the art, and they agree on the shape.

The finding that changes the build calculus

Chronon — Airbnb's declarative feature platform, which computes features once for both offline training and online low-latency serving — was first deployed at Airbnb specifically to combat payments fraud. It is now Apache 2.0. And Stripe integrated it: it powers 100% of Stripe's charge-path fraud prediction models.

A neobank building a fraud feature platform in 2026 can start from the same substrate two of the most sophisticated payments fraud teams in the world are running.

The durable split stays: buy case management, SAR filing and list data; build detection. Case management is workflow software and building it wins nothing. Detection is where your iteration speed is your loss rate.

Why the UK forces this early

Since 7 October 2024, UK payment firms must reimburse most authorised push payment fraud up to £85,000, split 50/50 between sending and receiving institution, with the burden on the firm to prove gross negligence and reimbursement within five working days. The regulator's July 2026 evaluation found APP losses over Faster Payments down about 21% — roughly £73m a year — with 97% of in-scope claims reimbursed.

Three architectural consequences, not policy ones:

  1. In-flight intervention, not post-hoc alerting. The control must warn, add friction or block before the payment leaves.
  2. Inbound mule detection is revenue. Receiving-side liability puts a direct P&L line on your inbound risk model — and mule networks are a graph problem, which is why Splink and Senzing appear in a fraud section at all.
  3. Evidence, because you carry the burden of proof. Proving gross negligence means showing the warning you displayed, when, and what the customer did next.

What is still missing

The numbers that would settle these arguments are not public, and no amount of searching produced them: cost per card authorisation at scale, a jPOS commercial licence at neobank volume, realistic false-positive rates for a mature monitoring stack, all-in cost per verified account. Neither Marble nor Jube publishes a production reference. No neobank publishes its break taxonomy or its auto-match rate by rail.

Those gaps are the standing research agenda — anyone who can supply a real number for any of them is contributing more than another vendor comparison.
