The shape of the decision

A founding team makes four choices. Everything else in the diagram above follows from them.

They are, in order of consequence: what are you licensed as, do you own the ledger, who authorises your card transactions, and is financial crime a vendor or a platform. Each one is usually presented as a procurement question. Each one is really an architecture question wearing a procurement question's clothes.

The licence decision is the load-bearing one, because it decides what a balance means. A deposit at a licensed bank is a liability on your own balance sheet. An e-money balance is a claim against safeguarded funds held elsewhere. A sponsor-bank fintech balance is a position in a sub-ledger inside somebody else's omnibus account. Identical UI; three completely different correctness requirements.

The strongest signal in the research

Two regulators, two legal systems, no coordination — and they arrived at the same requirement from opposite directions. Britain got there through safeguarding rules for e-money firms; America got there through a bankruptcy that left roughly $60–90m of customer money unaccounted for. Both now demand that reconciliation be continuous and provable.

That makes it the one subsystem to build first, in either market, whatever else you buy. No vendor can build it for you, because it encodes your specific relationships between your ledger, your rails and your partners.

The APP reimbursement divergence is the one that catches teams porting a US stack into the UK. It means a British neobank is liable for money its own customer chose to send to a fraudster — which turns outbound scam detection from a customer-service nicety into a P&L line, and makes inbound mule detection directly monetised via the receiving-side half of the split. It is why Monzo built in-flight intervention on the payment path rather than post-hoc alerting.

The hot path

Everything you want to know about a transaction must be knowable before the scheme times out.

Card authorisation is the only part of the stack with a hard external deadline, and it is the constraint that shapes the feature store, the control engine and the ledger's write path. Whatever risk scoring you want in-line has to fit inside the scheme's window minus the network — which is why the feature service is tiered by latency rather than by domain, and why "just add another model" is never free.

The subsystem nobody diagrams

Every layer holds an opinion about how much money exists. Reconciliation is what forces them to agree.

Build it as stateless, replayable workers — one per rail, one per partner — that read postings and external statements and emit breaks. A break is a ticket with an owner, an age and an amount. Unaged breaks are how shortfalls hide.

Surviving the cloud you run on

Every regulator asks the concentration question. Most firms answer with a document. One bank answered by building a second bank.

DORA Articles 28–30 and the FCA/PRA critical-third-parties regime require a documented, tested exit and continuity capability for critical providers. The honest problem is that a credible answer looks impossibly expensive — until you are ruthless about scope.

Two things to take from it even if you never build one. Continuity does not mean full function — pick the handful of things customers cannot live without and build only those, and the cost collapses to something arguable. And a failover you have never used is a hypothesis, not a control: real customers run on Stand-in permanently in testing mode, which is what turns it from a diagram into evidence.

The three triggers to reopen a "buy"

  1. The fee becomes your largest cost-to-serve line. Below a few million cards, buying wins. Above it, the arithmetic flips — and Chime's public claim of a cost-to-serve around a third of a large bank's is what the flipped side looks like.
  2. Their release train becomes your roadmap. If a product change takes two quarters because it needs a vendor change, the velocity argument is already lost.
  3. The vendor becomes a competitor or a regulatory risk. Galileo is owned by SoFi. Synapse went bankrupt with customer funds unreconciled. Concentration risk is now a documented requirement under DORA Articles 28–30, not a philosophical concern.

The questions that expose real problems

Twenty of the hundred. If you have an hour in a design review, ask these.

Licence & ledger

  1. Who holds the money, legally — whose balance sheet?
  2. If a sponsor bank is ordered to exit the programme, what is our migration path and how long is it?
  3. Is there a mutable balance field anywhere, or are balances derived from postings?
  4. Are the invariants enforced at the storage layer, or in application code that someone can bypass?
  5. Is every write idempotent, and what exactly is the deduplication key?
  6. Does product logic live as ledger transactions, or as application code that later posts?
  7. If bought: what is the migration cost away from their product-definition language?

Rails & cards

  1. Is there one normalised money-movement model, or per-rail special cases?
  2. Draw the payment state machine. Where can it hang, and for how long?
  3. What is the measured p99.9 authorisation latency, and how much budget is left for risk?
  4. What is the stand-in policy, what are the limits, and who carries the exposure?

Risk & decisioning

  1. Can a risk analyst ship a control without an engineer and without production access?
  2. Can a proposed control be back-tested against history before it goes live?
  3. Is every control execution logged with its input features, version and decision?
  4. Are credit and fincrime sharing one feature store, or computing "average monthly inflow" twice?
  5. Are training sets built point-in-time-correct from decision logs, or from current-state tables?
  6. How are adverse-action reason codes generated — and are they actually the reasons?

Proof

  1. Is reconciliation a first-class subsystem with a named owner, or a batch job?
  2. What is the oldest open break, and who owns it?
  3. Can the sponsor bank or regulator independently verify our sub-ledger, continuously?
  4. For each critical vendor: what is the documented exit strategy, and has it been rehearsed?

How this stays current

A wiki, four skills, and two jobs that run without being asked.

This page is the rendered output of a knowledge base that maintains itself. The research sits in an interlinked markdown wiki — ten layer pages, ten bank profiles, a vendor landscape, comparisons — with every non-obvious claim carrying a confidence tag: confirmed when a company or regulator said it, reported for credible third parties, inferred when it was deduced from job ads or vendor customer lists. Claims older than about eighteen months get dated, because stacks move and a 2020 fact driving a 2026 recommendation is the most dangerous thing in a document like this.
